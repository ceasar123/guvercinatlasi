Mardin Clap Tumbler (Takla) pigeon

Breed Atlas

A game pigeon raised in almost every part of Türkiye [4], one that dives toward its loft, tumbles, and shoots back up into the sky [1]. It is known internationally as Takla; one of its most common names in Türkiye is that of a city: Mardin [1].

History: a tumbler that carries a city's name

This bird's story begins not with a date but with a name. According to Savaş and Erdem, the breed known internationally as Takla is called by different names from region to region in Türkiye, and the most common of these are Mardin, oyun kuşu ("game bird"), and damcı [1]. A 2025 review puts the pigeon known as "Taklacı" [Tumbler] or "Mardin" at the head of the most commonly raised breeds in Türkiye [5]. An experimental paper published in 2026, on the same page, first calls the breed "Talka" ("also known as Takla") and then, when giving squab numbers, "Mardin"; it does not state outright that the two are the same breed [8].

According to a 2012 study compiling pigeon breeding in Batman, Diyarbakır, Mardin, and Şanlıurfa, tumbler-type pigeons are raised in Mardin, and pigeon breeding is a very important cultural element in the region. Among expert breeders, tumblers are known as "performance pigeons" [3].

The oldest dated text among the sources we read on the link between the name and the city comes from Konya. Mehmet Özmen, a Turkish language and literature assistant at Selçuk University, published in his 1981 article the pigeon-keeping vocabulary he collected from bird keepers in the center of Konya. According to his compilation, Konya's truly native pigeons were the Selçuklu type and the taklambaç; "Mardinli," on the other hand, was a type that was "sonradan geldiği herkesçe bilinen" [known by everyone to have come later] in the city, and had been brought to Konya from the Urfa and Mardin region. According to the article, the three types that were in the majority and kept by everyone in Konya were Selçuklu, Mardinli, and Taklambaç [2]. Özmen does not write when the birds arrived.

Today the bird does not fit into a single city. A 2012 compilation reports that Takla is raised mostly in Mardin, Şanlıurfa, Sivas, Ankara, Antalya, Diyarbakır, Malatya, and Konya [7]. According to a 2026 study comparing 22 native breeds, other breeds are tied to a particular geography, while there is almost no region where Takla is not raised [4]. The breed is also known abroad: in Europe it goes by the names "Turkish Clap Tumbler," in German "türkischer Klatschtümmler," and in French "le Claquer Culbutant Turquie" [1].

So where did the breed come from? Two articles describe Takla as a breed of Southeastern origin or native to Southeastern Anatolia [1] [6]. However, neither cites a source for this information; for that reason we relay it as the authors' assessment. The publications we read also do not point to a dated document showing when and where the breed appeared.

How to recognize it: first in the air

You recognize a Mardin not on the ground but in the sky. According to Savaş and Erdem's description, these birds dive toward their loft or the place where they will land at an angle of about 25 to 30 degrees, tumble when they reach the target, and then climb straight up, again tumbling. This steep climb is called "fişekleme" (roughly "rocketing") or "fışkırma" (roughly "spurting"). The birds can stay in the air for hours and can repeat this game many times; harsh wing and tumbling sounds are also among their distinguishing features [1]. A 2023 study describes the same movement this way: the bird approaches its loft in a nosedive, tumbles just before landing, then climbs back up perpendicular to the ground and keeps flying [6]. According to a 2012 compilation, tumbler pigeons also perform a certain number of consecutive tumbles forward or backward in the air, and a bird's quality is judged by the speed and number of these tumbles [7].

Özmen's 1981 dictionary recorded the language of this game. "Oyun" ("play") means the pigeon's tumbling, and it is seen "asıl olarak Mardinli türünde" [mainly in the Mardinli type]. While tumbling, the bird may change direction left and right; it pauses every three tumbles and does a "yenileme" ("renewal"). On average three renewals are made, and birds that change direction during the tumbles in between are more highly valued. The Mardinli does fişekleme; the Taklambaç also has play, but it does not do fişekleme, it "düz oynar" [plays straight]. A Mardinli that makes a lot of noise with its wings once it starts to play, climbs high, and makes many "tokat" ("slaps") while climbing is considered desirable; as slaps and height decrease, the bird's value falls. Birds called "havacı" ("aviator") can stay in the air for 3 to 4 hours on average; some stay as long as eight hours [2].

The 2012 Mardin compilation also lists the criteria that determine a tumbler's value: flying high and spread out, being a nosediver and a player, point diving, base tumbling, hanging quickly onto its fişek, making more than one tumble while hanging, leaving distance between tumbles, and a head-tying tumble at the finish. The bird is expected to come to the fişek every 10 to 15 minutes; wing sound, time in the air, and landing at its loft with tumbles also raise its value. According to enthusiasts, what makes the Mardin pigeon indispensable is the wing sounds of tumblers [3].

Why does a bird tumble in the air?

The answer to this question is not yet settled. This behavior is not seen in the pigeon's wild ancestor [6]. Üçtepe and colleagues, in their 2023 study, examined four female and four male birds of each breed, on the thought that the behavior might stem from differences in brain or cerebellum structure; Baska and homing pigeons were taken as controls. The brain of Takla (least squares mean) came to 1.65 g, Baska's to 1.84 g, and the homing pigeon's to 1.86 g: according to the authors, about 10 percent lighter on average. Moreover, Takla was heavier than Baska in live weight; according to the authors, it has a relatively small brain for its body size [6].

But no evidence was found that sets Takla apart from other pigeons in general brain tissue, and brain volume could not be measured. According to the authors, tumbling behavior develops with age; they write that comparing young and old birds could give a clearer result on brain shrinkage [6]. In other words, the study does not distinguish whether a small brain is the cause or the result of tumbling. The authors consider explaining the behavior only by an inner-ear balance disorder insufficient, and count hereditary tumbling among the "anomalies" that may negatively affect a bird's general welfare, especially as it gets older [6]; this is their interpretation.

Appearance: it varies by source

For appearance on the ground, the most detailed description we have again comes from Konya. According to Özmen, the Mardinli is a pigeon with a long beak, small eyes, a "silik" (indistinct) head, short paça (leg feathering), a long body, a neck of normal length, long toenails like a hawk's talons, and a straight tail. Its tail has at least 12 and at most 16 feathers. Its basic colors are ak (white), gök (bluish gray), and zidgara, that is, entirely black; but it can be any color. The back is light, while the head, neck, tail, and wing tips are darker. The head can be crested or crestless. The dictionary's summary is short: "Mardinli türünde renk önemli değildir. Oyun önemlidir." [In the Mardinli type, color is not important. Play is important.] The variety without leg feathering is called Çorumlu or Çorumlu-Mardinli, and the bird that falls within the Mardinli type but is larger and longer than it is called Musullu [2].

The Mardin compilation's description differs on some points. According to it, these tumblers are larger than other breeds; no crest or rose is seen in the pure Mardin tumbler, and because of mixing with other breeds, crested and rose-headed ones are also seen today. Their colors are black, white, blue, and smoky; the chest cage is also more showy [3]. Yet the 2026 experimental paper names the homing pigeon and the Dolapçı as breeds larger than Takla [8]. The 2025 compilation also notes that Takla's body, head, beak, feather, and flight characteristics may vary from region to region [5].

No published measurement of birds raised in Mardin turns up. Although tumblers in Ankara have been measured, Savaş and Erdem write that they merely guess those birds to be the breed called Takla, Mardin, or Oyun Kuşu, and remind us that the term "taklacı" [tumbler] covers many breeds [1].

Is Mardin a breed or a name?

The 2026 study compared 22 native breeds by coding 76 traits as present or absent. In this comparison Takla clearly stood apart from all other breeds; the authors find this consistent with the bird's unique flight performance and wide distribution [4]. The paper's methods section does not mention DNA data: the traits were coded from the authors' knowledge of the breeds, scientific studies, Ministry registration documents, and breeder information. According to the authors, this marked difference is also reflected in the breed's being called Mardin or Takla as well [4].

In the table, the Ankara measurement study and the brain study are cited as sources for Takla. The breed closest to Takla is Bayburt (relative distance 0.54), and the farthest are Dönek and Trabzon (0.70); the distance of breeds within groups to one another is between 0.07 and 0.26 [4]. The authors note that the accuracy of hierarchical clustering is limited, while multiple correspondence analysis offers a more reliable representation [4]. In other words, on paper, by its listed traits, Takla clearly stands apart from other breeds; its genetic distance cannot be inferred from this study.

The question of the name is still not settled. The 2025 compilation's table of native breeds lists "Mardin Taklacı" [Mardin Tumbler] on separate rows together with the Urfa and Sivas tumblers and the "Türk Taklacı" [Turkish Tumbler] [5]. In the 1981 Konya dictionary, Mardinli is the name of a type; Çorumlu is a variety of it [2]. The sources do not clearly separate whether the name Mardin describes a single breed or the tumbler type of the Mardin region. The same compilation names Mardin among the breeds that are numerous, and writes that these breeds have been unable to keep their purity [5].

The situation on the official side is also clear. According to Savaş and Erdem's 2026 study, the six pigeon breeds officially registered are Bursa, Hünkâri, Edremit Kelebek, Alabadem, Trakya, and Muğla; Takla is not on this list [4]. Savaş and Erdem wrote in 2021 that although a pigeon federation exists, there has been no initiative toward creating standards for the breeds in Türkiye [1]. In a news report in November 2022, bird lovers from Mardin voiced their wish for geographical registration for the tumbler pigeon [9]. As of November 15, 2026, no record under the name of pigeon appears in the Turkish Patent and Trademark Office's geographical indication database [10].
