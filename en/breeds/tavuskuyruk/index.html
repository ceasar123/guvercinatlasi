Turkish Fantail (Tavuskuyruk) pigeon

Breed Atlas

The fan-tailed pigeon in a book printed in Edinburgh in 1835, "Broad or Fan-tailed Shaker". The plate shows the European fantail group; it is not a description or an origin record of Turkish Tavuskuyruk birds. Prideaux John Selby, The Natural History of Pigeons (The Naturalist's Library), Edinburgh: W. H. Lizars, 1835, Plate XIII. Drawing: Edward Lear. Public domain. Image: Wellcome Collection.

In Yılmaz and Boz's 2012 compilation in Turkish, Tavuskuyruk is the ornamental pigeon given as the equivalent of the fantail in English, that is, pigeons with a fan-shaped tail. It is written to be raised nearly everywhere in the country and to be known by nine different names from region to region; but this information comes from a single compilation, and that in turn rests on a website.

Nine names, one source

The Turkish source we could find that defines Tavuskuyruk is very short. According to Yılmaz and Boz's 2012 compilation, this ornamental breed is raised for show, is known in English as the Fantail, and is found in nearly every region of the country. Depending on the locality where it is raised, it may be called Teskel, Tersgel, Hindi, Sepetkuyruk, Fenerkuyruk, Ganrık, Rakkas, Kırkkuyruk, or Hendavi [1]. The compilation bases this information on a pigeon website accessed in 2010 and does not say which name is used in which locality [1]. We do not speculate about what the names mean.

One of the names is also recorded in the field. In fieldwork with bird fanciers (kuşçu) in Ankara and Urfa, Yazıcı Yakın mentions, among the birds taken out of their coops, the Fenerkuyruks "karmaşık tüy yığınını halinde gezen" (in the original wording) ["walking about as a complex heap of feathers"] [5]. The information that Fenerkuyruk is a name of Tavuskuyruk, however, does not come from that study but from Yılmaz and Boz's list [1].

The list of names overlaps with another breed in one place: Kırkkuyruk is also a local name given for Gümüşkuyruk in the same compilation [1].

The fan tail in European books

The fan-tailed pigeon is described in detail in the old pigeon books of Europe. In 1824 in Paris, Boitard and Corbié showed it under the name "pigeon trembleur paon," that is, the trembling peacock pigeon, on plate 23 [9]. In 1835 in Edinburgh, Selby gave the same bird as the "Broad or Fan-tailed Shaker" on Plate XIII and cited page 224 of Boitard [11].

According to Darwin, the first source to mention this breed is the Ayeen Akbery, written in India before 1600; at that date, judging by Aldrovandi, this breed was not a known breed in Europe [8]. The 2012 genetic study also writes that fantail breeds probably arose in India; but it gives this not as its own finding, but by citing Levi's 1986 book [12]. In 1883, La Perre de Roo, for his part, claimed that these birds were very widespread in Asia, from Asiatic Turkey to the Far East, from İzmir to the Philippines [10].

These books describe the international fantail group. None of them mentions the name Tavuskuyruk, and none shows that the birds in Türkiye descend from this stock.

The bird as drawn: the fantail in European sources

The Turkish sources we surveyed do not describe the appearance of Tavuskuyruk; the number of tail feathers, body size, and color varieties are not given either. What they do say is this: in ornamental pigeons performance is not considered, and the birds are raised only for their outward appearance [1]; among the features that bring this group to the fore, "daha gösterişli kuyruk" ["a more showy tail"] is also listed, and Tavuskuyruk is among the examples of the group [2]; but this is a group definition, and it does not describe Tavuskuyruk's own tail.

What follows is therefore not a description of the Turkish bird, but of the fantail in 19th-century European books:

  • Tail: According to Darwin, in the European fantail the tail is open, directed upward, and has many feathers [8]. According to Darwin, the usual number of tail feathers in the genus pigeon is 12 [8]; according to Boitard, in the fantail it is 28 to 42 [9]. Darwin counted 33 in one of his own birds, and 22 and 27 in two young from the same nest [8].
  • Carriage: According to Darwin, the neck is thin and curved backward, and the chest is broad and prominent [8]. In good birds the head touches the tail feathers [8]; Boitard also writes that the bird raising its tail throws its head back and touches it to the tail [9].
  • Trembling: The birds tremble often; their necks have a backward and forward movement that Darwin calls "apparently convulsive" [8].
  • Size: According to Selby, smaller than most varieties; the beak is short and slender, the wings droop, and the legs and feet are unfeathered [11].
  • Color: According to Selby, the most frequent appearance is entirely white, or black on the head and tail with the rest white [11]; Boitard also lists these two states, but both authors add that other colors are also seen [9] [11].
  • Hen: According to Boitard and Selby, she can spread and raise her tail as much as the male [9] [11].

According to Darwin, the permanent fan-like spread of the feathers and their upward direction are more striking features than the increase in their number [8]. La Perre de Roo holds the same view: the finest birds he saw had 34 feathers, and in his view it was the carriage of the tail that gave the bird its value [10].

What changed inside the tail

Darwin did not study these birds only from the outside. In many fantails from different countries he found no trace of the oil gland that is present in most birds [8]. The number of tail vertebrae, which is seven in the rock pigeon, was eight or nine in the fantails Darwin examined, and perhaps ten in one specimen [8]. According to Darwin, during the process of selection the internal skeleton and the outward form of the body had changed, to some extent, in a correlated way [8]. In 2022, Savaş and Erdem also write that in some breeds such as the Indian fantail, targeted selection was carried out for a showy tail and that this can lengthen the tail [7].

A short tour of the sky

The old European books agree that the fantail does not fly well: it can be caught by the wind because of its broad tail [9], it flies clumsily [11], it flies badly on a windy day [8]. In a coffeehouse conversation in Ankara, a bird fancier talks about the birds of someone named M. (the researcher adds that these are dönek and fenerkuyruk): when thrown into the air, they fly for at most ten minutes and land again; a tumbler (taklacı), once it goes up, does not come down [5]. This is the account of a single bird fancier; and the information that Fenerkuyruk is a name of Tavuskuyruk also comes only from Yılmaz and Boz's list [1].

A breed or a tail type?

The name Tavuskuyruk appears in several Turkish breed lists. A 2014 compilation gives it as an example of ornamental breeds [2]. A 2024 article writes that the breeds in Türkiye are divided into nine main groups (without saying who divided them) and places Gümüşkuyruk, Hünkâri, Karakan, Selçuklu, and Tavuskuyruk in the form and ornamental group; according to the authors, breeds in Türkiye, unlike in other countries, are classified by flight display; they base this last sentence on three studies that include Yılmaz and Boz 2012 [3]. A 2025 compilation counts Tavuskuyruk among the breeds in Türkiye; elsewhere it also mentions the name "Tavus" among the pigeons seen in greater numbers and adds that these breeds could not keep their purity [4]. It is not written explicitly that "Tavus" and Tavuskuyruk are the same bird.

The problem is that the word "fantail" does not belong to a single Turkish breed. In the same 2012 compilation, the English name of Gümüşkuyruk is Karakand Fantail or Syrian Fantail, and it has 16 to 20 tail feathers [1]. Karakan is also locally called Yelpaze Kuyruk, and its feather count ranges from 16 to 22 [1]. One of Selçuklu's English names is also Seljuk Fantail [1]. In the 2025 compilation's classification of form pigeons there is also a subgroup called "deste kuyruklar" ["bundle tails"]; its relation to Tavuskuyruk is not stated [4]. Whether the nine local names in a single compilation, one that rests on a website, truly belong to a single breed or to several local types cannot be understood from the publications we covered.

In 2026, Savaş and Erdem compared 22 local breeds in terms of behavior, appearance, and some genetic characteristics; Tavuskuyruk is not on this list [6]. The six native pigeon breeds that the same authors count as officially registered are Bursa, Hünkâri, Edremit Kelebek, Alabadem, Trakya, and Muğla [6]. Tavuskuyruk is also absent from the current text of the communiqué on the registration of native animal breeds [13].

In short, Tavuskuyruk is a bird that appears in several Turkish breed lists but that, in the written sources we covered, stands only as a short list of names. The gaps are collected in the Open file below.

What the sources say with certainty about this breed, and questions not yet studied.

Known

  • Yılmaz and Boz's 2012 compilation gives Tavuskuyruk as an ornamental breed raised for show, with the English name Fantail and known by nine local names; this information rests on a pigeon website accessed in 2010 [1].
  • The name Kırkkuyruk is given in the same compilation for both Tavuskuyruk and Gümüşkuyruk [1]. The name Fenerkuyruk is also mentioned in a field study, but the information that Fenerkuyruk is a name of Tavuskuyruk comes only from Yılmaz and Boz [5] [1].
  • The name Tavuskuyruk appears in Turkish breed lists of 2014, 2024, and 2025 [2] [3] [4]; it is not among the 22 local breeds that Savaş and Erdem compared in 2026 [6].
  • Tavuskuyruk is not on the official registration list [6] [13].
  • Boitard and Corbié (1824), Selby (1835), and Darwin (1875) describe the fan-tailed pigeon in detail; none of them mentions the name Tavuskuyruk, and none shows that the birds in Türkiye descend from this stock [9] [11] [8].

Not yet studied

  • Measurements and genetics: No published measurement or genetic comparison was found for Tavuskuyruk in Türkiye. The number of tail feathers, body size, and color varieties are therefore known only from European books; Tavuskuyruk is not in Savaş and Erdem's 2026 comparison, which also covers some genetic characteristics [6].
  • Regional distribution of local names: The nine names depend on a single compilation and the website it rests on; which name is used in which locality is not stated [1]. No source on the meaning of the names was found. We could not access the full text of Yılmaz and colleagues' 2013 inventory of pigeon breeds in Türkiye; the Tavuskuyruk entry therefore could not be checked.
  • Relation to Gümüşkuyruk, Karakan, Selçuklu, and Deste Kuyruk: These three types are called by the name fantail in the same compilation [1]; the relation of the "deste kuyruklar" subgroup in the 2025 compilation to Tavuskuyruk is not stated [4]. Whether the nine names belong to a single breed or to several local types cannot be understood from the publications we covered. Whether "Tavus" and Tavuskuyruk are the same bird is also not written [4].
  • Origin: No published document showing when and from where Tavuskuyruk came to Türkiye was found.
  • Standard and registration: In the sources we covered there is no federation or association standard for Tavuskuyruk; there is no registration record either [13].
